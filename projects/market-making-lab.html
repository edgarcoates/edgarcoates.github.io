Market-Making Lab

A simulated market in which a trading agent learns how to quote prices. It runs in your browser, and you can quote against it yourself.

Why this project. At Tradeweb I worked on how dealers and clients trade on an electronic venue, at Minerva I manage the risk in a portfolio, and I build AI tools. A dealer quoting a two-way price needs all three, and like poker, which I study, it is a decision made against people who may know more than you.

    The problem

    A market maker quotes a price to buy and a price to sell. When both trade, it keeps the gap between them.

    Two things reduce that profit. Some traders know more than the market maker and trade only when its quote is wrong, so it loses on those trades. This is adverse selection. And buys and sells do not arrive in pairs, so the market maker is left holding a position while the price moves. This is inventory risk.

    A tight quote trades more often and loses more to informed traders. A wide quote is safer and rarely trades. This project measures that trade-off in a small market with fixed rules.

    The order book

    The best price trades first. At the same price, the oldest order trades first. This is price-time priority, the rule most electronic order books use.

    The true value

    It moves at random each step, and the market maker only sees the move afterwards. Everyone quotes around the previous step's value, called P.

    Calm and volatile periods

    The market switches at random between the two. In a volatile period the value moves about 2.4 times as far each step, so quotes go out of date faster.

    Informed traders

    40% of arriving traders know the new value. They trade only when a quote is on the wrong side of it.

    Uninformed traders

    The other 60% buy or sell at random. The closer a quote is to P, the more likely they are to take it.

    Competing dealers

    They always quote 3 ticks from P in large size. A market maker quoting 3 ticks waits behind them and almost never trades.

    The agent

    It sees two things: its inventory, and whether the market is calm or volatile.

    Each step the agent picks a bid and an ask, each 1, 2 or 3 ticks from P. That gives nine possible quotes. After the step it gets a score: its profit on any trades, measured against the new value, minus a penalty that grows with the square of its inventory.

    It starts by quoting at random. Over 20,000 practice episodes it keeps a running score for every quote in every situation, and picks the best-scoring quote more and more often. The method is tabular Q-learning.

    What it learned.

    With no position in a calm market, it quotes 2 ticks each side.

    When it holds a position, it moves the quote that would close the position to 1 tick. With a larger position it also moves the other quote further away. If it is long, its next trade is more likely to be a sale.

    In a volatile market with no position, it quotes 3 ticks each side. That puts it behind the dealers, so it stops trading until the market is calm again. If it is holding a position, it still quotes 1 tick on the side that closes it.

    Market-making theory recommends both habits: shift quotes against your inventory, and widen them when volatility rises. The agent was given neither rule.

    Results

    Every strategy traded the same 5,000 simulated episodes. Figures are ticks per 200-step episode. "Lost" is the share of episodes that ended below zero.

    Fixed quotes carry the most risk. The 2-tick quote averages 15.2 ticks an episode with a standard deviation of 89, and loses money in 36% of episodes. The 1-tick quote trades most often and earns least.

    Seeing volatility made the biggest difference. The agent that sees only its inventory averages 10.2 ticks. The agent that also sees volatility averages 15.4, the highest of the six, with a standard deviation of 13. It loses money in 9% of episodes.

    A hand-written rule still does slightly better for the risk taken. My rule that leans against inventory and steps back in volatile periods scores 1.29 on mean divided by standard deviation. The agent scores 1.18. The agent earns about 15% more on average, and its results vary about 27% more.

    Limits

    This is a simulated market. It uses no market data, and the results are in ticks, not money. The results describe how the trade-off behaves under the assumptions below, and say nothing about how any strategy would do in a real market.

    The market
    One instrument and one-lot orders. The competing dealers never change their quotes.
    The book
    Quotes are replaced every step, so a place in the queue does not carry over to the next step.
    The agent
    It is told whether the market is calm or volatile. A real trader would have to estimate that from recent price moves. A table works here because there are only 26 situations and 9 quotes.
    The measure
    The size of the inventory penalty is a number I chose. A different value gives a different balance of profit and risk.
