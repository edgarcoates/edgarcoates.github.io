    The problem

    A market maker quotes a price to buy and a price to sell, and earns the gap when both trade. Two things take that gap away.

    Adverse selection. Some traders know more than the quote does. They trade only when the quote is wrong, so every fill from them is a loss.

    Inventory risk. Buys and sells don't arrive in pairs. The market maker ends up holding a position while the price moves.

    Quote tight and you trade more, but you are picked off more and build inventory faster. Quote wide and you are safe but idle. The lab is a small market where that trade-off can be measured.

    The order book

    Price-time priority: the best price trades first, and at the same price the oldest order trades first. Orders can rest, cross, walk several levels, or be immediate-or-cancel.

    The true value

    A random walk the market maker cannot see until the next step. Everyone quotes around last step's value, rounded to a tick.

    Informed takers

    40% of arrivals know the new value. They trade only when a resting quote is on the wrong side of it.

    Uninformed takers

    The rest pick a side at random and pay up to a random distance from the reference price, so a tighter quote is hit more often.

    Competing dealers

    Always quoting three ticks away in size. Quote there and you sit behind them in the queue, which in practice means no fills.

    The market maker

    Posts one bid and one ask each step, each one, two or three ticks from the reference price. Nine possible quotes.

    The agent

    Tabular Q-learning. It sees one thing: its own inventory.

    The agent starts knowing nothing and quoting at random. After each step it is rewarded with the edge on its fills against the new value, less a penalty that grows with the square of its inventory. Over 10,000 episodes of 200 steps it builds a table of how good each of the nine quotes is at each inventory level, and explores less as the table fills in.

    Showing the agent trained for the results below. Train a new one here: it takes a few seconds, and you can change how much of the flow is informed first.

    What it learned. When flat, quote two ticks each side. As soon as it is long by one unit, pull the ask in to one tick and push the bid out to three, so the next trade is more likely to be a sale. Mirror that when short. It gives up edge on the side that reduces the position and stops adding on the other.

    That is the same idea as the standard inventory models of market making, where the quote is centred on a price shifted against the position. Nobody told the agent; it got there from the reward.

    The outer rows of the chart (inventory beyond about three) are states the trained agent almost never reaches, so its choices there are not reliable.

    Results

    Every strategy faced the same 5,000 simulated episodes. Figures are ticks per 200-step episode. "Lost" is the share of episodes ending below zero.

    StrategyMean profitStd devMean ÷ sd5th pctLostAvg |inv.|

    The tightest quote is the worst. One tick each side gets the most fills and the lowest profit, because it is the quote informed traders can pick off most often.

    The fixed 2-tick quote earns the most on average and is the riskiest way to earn it. It never manages its position, so its result swings widely and it loses money in about three episodes in ten.

    The trained agent gives up about 30% of that average profit and removes about 80% of the variation. It loses money in one episode in eight, and its bad outcomes are far milder: a 5th-percentile result of −7.5 ticks against −99.8.

    It did not beat a well-tuned rule. My hand-written rule waits until inventory reaches two units before skewing. Moving that threshold to one unit reproduces the agent's results exactly. What the agent added was finding the threshold without being given it.

    Trained agent's mean profit per episode as informed flow rises, in ticks

    A separate agent was trained at each level and tested on 2,000 episodes. As more of the flow is informed, the spread the market maker can keep shrinks. At 60% the agent stopped quoting inside the dealers and made nothing.

    Limits

    This is a simulated market, not market data, and the results are in ticks, not money. They show how the trade-off behaves under the assumptions below. They say nothing about how any strategy would do in a real market.

    The market
    One instrument, one-lot orders, a value that follows a simple random walk. The competing dealers never adapt.
    The book
    Quotes are replaced every step, so queue position does not carry over from one step to the next.
    The agent
    It sees only its own inventory: no order flow, volatility or time of day. A table works because there are 13 states and 9 actions; a richer state would need function approximation.
    The measure
    Mean divided by standard deviation of profit per episode. The inventory penalty that produces the agent's caution is a parameter I chose, and a different value gives a different trade-off.
